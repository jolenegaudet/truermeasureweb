# Part 7 — Nobody decided

*A written piece in the founder's voice, answering her own question: when did the report card become the document parents rely on? Drafted 2026-09-08. Dates checked against the sources listed at the end; where historians disagree, the text says "around." The founder's series, Parts 1–6, is hers and untouched; this is a proposed addition for her to make hers or discard.*

---

## The piece

Nobody decided the report card was the truth about your child.

It just became the only thing in writing.

Here's roughly how that happened.

For most of history, there was no document. A teacher knew the family. Learning was shown — a child read aloud, recited, stood up in front of the room. If you wanted to know how your child was doing, you asked the person who taught them. The conversation *was* the report.

Then schools got big.

Around 1848, a school in Boston put one teacher in one room with children all the same age, and the model spread across the continent. A teacher who had known twenty families now had forty children and a new set of them every September. The conversation didn't scale.

So the document arrived in its place. In the 1830s and 40s, teachers in the new common schools started sending home short notes — mostly about conduct, mostly to get parents on side. By the 1870s those notes had become printed forms, sold by companies, required by superintendents. Somewhere in there the phrase "report card" appears in a state superintendent's annual report. It was an administrative solution to an administrative problem: too many children, too little time, and a home that needed to hear something.

Notice what it was never designed to do. It wasn't designed to describe a child. It was designed to *report* — to an institution's standard, at an institution's scale.

Then the grades came, and they came from a different place entirely.

Universities had been ranking students for a century. Yale's president was sorting his students into four tiers in the 1780s — best, second best, worse, worst. The point was rank. Who is ahead of whom. Nobody was trying to capture a person; they were trying to order a list.

The letters we use now — A, B, C — show up around 1897, at a women's college in Massachusetts. And they spread for a reason that should make every parent pause.

In 1912 and 1913, two researchers gave the same English paper to 142 teachers and asked them to mark it. The marks ranged from 64 to 98. Then they did it with a geometry paper and 128 math teachers. The marks ranged from 28 to 95. The same work. The difference was the teacher.

The response wasn't to describe learning better. It was to make the marks more *consistent*. Fewer categories. A to F. A number that would come out the same no matter who gave it.

That's the century the report card was built in — the one that ran schools like factories on purpose, because it was the age that ran everything like a factory on purpose. Reliability was the goal. Comparability was the goal. And the thing about reliability is that it is the enemy of description. Everything you'd want to know about your child is variance. The form was built to remove it.

Then the document got attached to consequences. Promotion. Credits. Admission. Once the paper decided things, it acquired an authority the conversation never had.

So when did the report card become what parents rely on?

When it became the only written, official thing a parent ever receives about their child, from an institution, tied to what happens next.

Written beats remembered.

Official beats observed.

That's the whole answer.

And it means the incompleteness isn't carelessness. It's design. The report card is very good at what it was built for: telling a school, reliably, where a child sits against the school's list. It was never built to tell you who your child is.

Nobody's going to fix that from inside the form. The form is doing its job.

Which leaves the other column — everything you see, everything a coach or a grandparent or a Tuesday afternoon shows you — with nowhere official to go.

For a hundred and fifty years, that column has lived in a parent's memory.

I think it deserves to be written down too.

## What's asserted, and how sure

| Claim in the piece | Basis | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One teacher, one grade per room from ~1848 (Quincy Grammar School, Boston) | Multiple histories of the graded school | Solid; "around" used |
| Report cards began 1830s–40s in common schools as conduct/parent-communication tools; standardized printed forms by the 1870s; term "report card" in an 1869 Wisconsin superintendent's report | Histories of the report card | Reasonable; exact firsts are contested, so the text says "somewhere in there" |
| Yale's president ranking students into four tiers in the 1780s (Ezra Stiles, 1785) | Stiles's diary, widely cited (Guskey; Durm) | Solid |
| Letter grades A–E at Mount Holyoke, 1897 | Mount Holyoke's own archives | Solid |
| Starch & Elliott: 1912 English paper, 142 teachers, 64–98 (second paper 50–97); 1913 geometry, 128 teachers, 28–95 | The original School Review papers, cited in Guskey and many since | Solid |
| "Ran schools like factories on purpose" | Callahan, *Education and the Cult of Efficiency* (1962); Tyack, *The One Best System* (1974) | Interpretive but mainstream |
| "Reliability is the enemy of description" | The founder's argument, not a citation | Stated as her view |
